About me

Lena is a PhD researcher in the Social AI Group at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. Her research focuses on the child-centred design of socially intelligent AI systems, particularly social robots, with an emphasis on education, long-term interaction, and responsible AI.

She specialises in addressing real-world challenges with innovative, socially meaningful and responsible interaction designs. Her work combines generative AI, participatory design, and empirical evaluation, working closely with schools and involving children as active contributors to the design of technology.

Throughout her PhD, Lena has developed and led research addressing several interconnected challenges:

  • Meaningful educational applications: In Robot Bookworm, which she co-led with her advisor, Dr Mike Ligthart, she explored how personalized child–robot interactions can support children’s reading motivation, bringing together generative AI, educational goals, and real-world school deployments.
  • Long-term interaction and relationships: In Robot Reconnect, Lena worked with a team of students to investigate how social robots can reconnect with children after extended periods of separation, supporting relational and informational continuity over time.
  • Responsible and child-centred AI: Lena initiated and led the Open Robot Memory project, working with a team of students to design and evaluate ways for children to inspect, challenge, and influence what social robots remember about them.

Across these projects, a central theme of Lena’s research is moving beyond AI systems that simply adapt to children, towards AI systems that children can understand, influence, and actively help shape.

Open Robot Memory Open Minded Robots

This is my most recent research, carried out over the past year. Social robots that personalise conversations store information about the children they talk to, but children rarely get to see that information, let alone change it. In this project, we open up the robot's memory to them.

First, children helped us design how this should work. Then 71 children aged 10–12 talked one-on-one with a NAO robot about school life and their dreams for the future. At any moment they could ask what the robot remembered about them and change it in their own words. Some of them also had a Memory Book on a tablet that showed what the robot knew.

To see how children react when the robot gets them wrong, it made a few deliberate mistakes, like saying their favourite food was broccoli.

What I'm asking

  • How do children understand what a robot remembers about them?
  • How do they respond when it gets something about them wrong?
  • When they try to change its memory, does the robot actually pick it up?

Open Robot Memory

Letting children see and change what a robot remembers

Together with children, we co-designed a Memory Book and ways for a robot to talk about what it remembers. Then 71 children aged 10–12 talked with the robot and could ask about and correct what it knew about them, including a few deliberate mistakes.

The Robot Bookworm

Personalised book talks with a social robot

A social robot that discusses books with children to support their reading motivation. Its conversations are generated with language models through a hybrid human–AI approach that keeps the content safe and personal.

Fitting Humor

Robot humour that fits a child's age

Co-design workshops with 102 children aged 8–12 identified the humour that suits each age group. In a follow-up study, children found the robot funnier and more relatable when its humour matched their age.
