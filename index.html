About me

Lena is a PhD researcher in the Social AI Group at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. Her research focuses on the child-centred design of socially intelligent AI systems, particularly social robots, with an emphasis on education, long-term interaction, and responsible AI.

She specialises in addressing real-world challenges with innovative, socially meaningful and responsible interaction designs. Her work combines generative AI, participatory design, and empirical evaluation, working closely with schools and involving children as active contributors to the design of technology.

Throughout her PhD, Lena has developed and led research addressing several interconnected challenges:

  • Meaningful educational applications: In Robot Bookworm, which she co-led with her advisor, Dr Mike Ligthart, she explored how personalized child–robot interactions can support children’s reading motivation, bringing together generative AI, educational goals, and real-world school deployments.
  • Long-term interaction and relationships: In Robot Reconnect, Lena worked with a team of students to investigate how social robots can reconnect with children after extended periods of separation, supporting relational and informational continuity over time.
  • Responsible and child-centred AI: Lena initiated and led the Open Robot Memory project, working with a team of students to design and evaluate ways for children to inspect, challenge, and influence what social robots remember about them.

Across these projects, a central theme of Lena’s research is moving beyond AI systems that simply adapt to children, towards AI systems that children can understand, influence, and actively help shape.

Open Robot Memory formerly Open-Minded Robots

Giving children insight into and influence over what social robots remember about them.

Social robots that personalize their conversations remember information about the children they interact with. But children rarely get to see what these robots remember, let alone correct it.

Drawing on my experiences from previous studies with children in schools, I began to question this one-sided approach to personalization. Could we design child-facing AI systems that are more transparent and give children meaningful influence over what these systems remember about them?

This question became the starting point for Open Robot Memory.

From research idea to working system

Over the past year, together with a wonderful team of BSc and MSc thesis students, we developed and evaluated this idea in Dutch primary schools. These schools have been valued research partners for several years, welcoming me back for new studies with our social robot, Leo. Some participating children had interacted with Leo before, while others were meeting him for the first time.

Bringing this system to life was a real team effort. Following a child-centred, participatory design approach, we began by exploring children's understanding of what it means for a robot to remember things about them. Through co-design activities, children shared how they imagined a robot's memory, what they thought it should remember, and how they would like to interact with it.

We translated these ideas into a system that allowed children to inspect, question, and correct the robot's memory through natural conversation, using their own words.

Putting the design to the test

We evaluated our design in a study with 71 children aged 10–12 from three classes at two Dutch primary schools. During one-on-one conversations with Leo about school life, personal interests, and future aspirations, children could ask what the robot remembered about them and correct that information in their own words.

Some children also had access to the Memory Book, a tablet-based interface implemented by Julianna, allowing them to inspect the robot's memory and check whether their corrections had been registered.

To explore how children respond when a robot gets something wrong, we deliberately introduced a few wrong claims into the conversations. We investigated when and how children challenged these mistakes, whether the robot successfully incorporated their corrections, and how making its memory visible influenced children's understanding and sense of agency.

Along the way, we also discovered that broccoli wasn't anyone's favourite food, and becoming a school principal wasn't a particularly popular career ambition either. :)

What we investigated

  • How do children understand what a robot remembers about them?
  • How do they respond when the robot gets something wrong?
  • When children try to correct its memory, does the robot successfully register those corrections?
  • How does making robot memory visible influence children's understanding and sense of agency?

Looking ahead

Ultimately, this project explores how we can move beyond AI systems that simply remember information about children, towards systems whose memories children can understand, question, and actively shape.

I am very proud of Julianna, Eunike, Sherissa, and Sander, each of whom developed an excellent thesis as part of this project. I am also very proud that Open Robot Memory is my first largely independent scientific initiative and research project. We are currently working on publishing the results of our research.

Robot Reconnect Keeping child–robot relationships alive over time

How can a social robot reconnect with children after a long break — without the physical robot being there?

A personalized robot may remember a child from one interaction to the next, but what happens when a year passes? Children's interests change, some things are forgotten, and the relationship itself may need renewing. Following our Robot Bookworm study, I wanted to explore how we could sustain both the social connection and the robot's knowledge of the child over time.

Together with a team of BSc students, we designed a practical way for children to reconnect with Leo without bringing the physical robot back into the classroom.

A year later, Leo says hello again

51 children who had previously met Leo in the Robot Bookworm study took part in this follow-up. Each child experienced two personalized forms of reconnection: a paper letter written in Leo's voice and a web-based chat with him. Both drew on what Leo had learned about the child's interests and preferences during the earlier study.

The interactions were designed to make children feel remembered while also giving them opportunities to correct outdated details, share new information, and refresh the robot's memory. We investigated children's sense of connection, their experiences with the two modalities, and how well the reconnection helped recover or update personal information.

From reconnecting to open memory

Beyond exploring how to reconnect with a familiar robot, the study revealed the importance of keeping a robot's knowledge up to date and allowing children to influence what it remembers. These questions helped motivate the next step in my research: Open Robot Memory.

The study also supported two excellent BSc thesis projects. We are working on sharing the research through a scientific publication.

Fitting Humor

Robot humour that fits a child's age
