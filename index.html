About me

I am a PhD researcher in the Social AI Group at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam. I study how to design socially intelligent AI systems for children—especially social robots—with a focus on education, long-term interaction and responsible AI.

My work combines generative AI, participatory design and empirical studies in real schools. I enjoy developing new interaction concepts, but I care just as much about whether they are meaningful to children—and whether children have a genuine say in the technology built for them.

My PhD is supervised by Dr Mike E.U. Ligthart and Prof. Koen V. Hindriks at VU Amsterdam, and Dr Shenghui Wang at the University of Twente. Their complementary perspectives on child–robot interaction, social AI, and language technologies help connect the human and technical sides of my research.

Research community

Part of the Hybrid Intelligence consortium

I feel fortunate and proud to be part of the Hybrid Intelligence (HI) consortium, a long-term collaboration among Dutch universities exploring how human and artificial intelligence can work together. The consortium is supported by the NWO Gravitation Programme (grant 024.004.022, 2019–2029). Its commitment to collaborative, adaptive, responsible and explainable AI is closely aligned with my own research.

Across my PhD, I have explored three connected challenges:

  • Meaningful educational applications: In Robot Bookworm, which I co-led with Dr Mike Ligthart, we explored how personalized conversations with a robot might make reading more enjoyable and deepen children's connection to books.
  • Long-term interaction and relationships: I initiated and led Robot Reconnect, supervising two BSc thesis students as we explored how a robot can reconnect with children after a long break—and keep its knowledge of them up to date.
  • Responsible and child-centred AI: In Open Robot Memory, a project I initiated and led, we designed ways for children to inspect, question and change what a robot remembers about them.

These projects share a common ambition: to move beyond AI systems that merely adapt to children, towards systems children can understand, challenge and actively shape.

Open Robot Memory formerly Open-Minded Robots

Giving children insight into and influence over what social robots remember about them.

Social robots that personalize their conversations remember information about the children they interact with. But children rarely get to see what these robots remember, let alone correct it.

Drawing on my experiences from previous studies with children in schools, I began to question this one-sided approach to personalization. Could we design child-facing AI systems that are more transparent and give children meaningful influence over what these systems remember about them?

This question became the starting point for Open Robot Memory.

From research idea to working system

Over the past year, together with a wonderful team of BSc and MSc thesis students, we developed and evaluated this idea in Dutch primary schools. These schools have been valued research partners for several years, welcoming me back for new studies with our social robot, Leo. Some participating children had interacted with Leo before, while others were meeting him for the first time.

Bringing this system to life was a real team effort. Following a child-centred, participatory design approach, we began by exploring children's understanding of what it means for a robot to remember things about them. Through co-design activities, children shared how they imagined a robot's memory, what they thought it should remember, and how they would like to interact with it.

We translated these ideas into a system that allowed children to inspect, question, and correct the robot's memory through natural conversation, using their own words.

Putting the design to the test

We evaluated our design in a study with 71 children aged 10–12 from three classes at two Dutch primary schools. During one-on-one conversations with Leo about school life, personal interests, and future aspirations, children could ask what the robot remembered about them and correct that information in their own words.

Some children also had access to the Memory Book, a tablet-based interface implemented by Julianna, allowing them to inspect the robot's memory and check whether their corrections had been registered.

To explore how children respond when a robot gets something wrong, we deliberately introduced a few wrong claims into the conversations. We investigated when and how children challenged these mistakes, whether the robot successfully incorporated their corrections, and how making its memory visible influenced children's understanding and sense of agency.

Along the way, we also discovered that broccoli wasn't anyone's favourite food, and becoming a school principal wasn't a particularly popular career ambition either. :)

What we investigated

  • How do children understand what a robot remembers about them?
  • How do they respond when the robot gets something wrong?
  • When children try to correct its memory, does the robot successfully register those corrections?
  • How does making robot memory visible influence children's understanding and sense of agency?

Looking ahead

Ultimately, this project explores how we can move beyond AI systems that simply remember information about children, towards systems whose memories children can understand, question, and actively shape.

I am very proud of Julianna, Eunike, Sherissa, and Sander, each of whom developed an excellent thesis as part of this project. I am also very proud that Open Robot Memory is my first largely independent scientific initiative and research project. We are currently working on publishing the results of our research.

Robot Reconnect Keeping child–robot relationships alive over time

How can a social robot reconnect with children after a long break — without the physical robot being there?

A personalized robot may remember a child from one interaction to the next, but what happens when a year passes? Children's interests change, some things are forgotten, and the relationship itself may need renewing. Following our Robot Bookworm study, I wanted to explore how we could sustain both the social connection and the robot's knowledge of the child over time.

Together with a team of BSc students, we designed a practical way for children to reconnect with Leo without bringing the physical robot back into the classroom.

A year later, Leo says hello again

51 children who had previously met Leo in the Robot Bookworm study took part in this follow-up. Each child experienced two personalized forms of reconnection: a paper letter written in Leo's voice and a web-based chat with him. Both drew on what Leo had learned about the child's interests and preferences during the earlier study.

The interactions were designed to make children feel remembered while also giving them opportunities to correct outdated details, share new information, and refresh the robot's memory. We investigated children's sense of connection, their experiences with the two modalities, and how well the reconnection helped recover or update personal information.

From reconnecting to open memory

Beyond exploring how to reconnect with a familiar robot, the study revealed the importance of keeping a robot's knowledge up to date and allowing children to influence what it remembers. These questions helped motivate the next step in my research: Open Robot Memory.

The study also supported two excellent BSc thesis projects. We are working on sharing the research through a scientific publication.

The Robot Bookworm Personalized reading support

What if a robot could be a reading companion rather than another teacher?

Just before I began my PhD, my supervisor, Dr Mike E.U. Ligthart, invited me to join Robot Bookworm. Its original research team had come together through an interdisciplinary pilot supported by the VU Network Institute's Academy Assistants programme. Mike's idea was to support children's reading motivation through personalized, relatable conversations about books with a social robot. I gladly joined: the prospect of a robot that could be a curious reading companion, rather than deliver another lesson, was exactly the kind of research I wanted to pursue.

I was fortunate that Mike gave me considerable scientific freedom to help shape the project. The primary schools where I had conducted my MSc thesis research also warmly welcomed us back to carry out the study. Mike and I co-led Robot Bookworm with an enthusiastic student team: Adriana Cabrera and Sobhaan Ul Husan, together with Kuhu Sinha, Sofie Veld, Rafaella van Nee, and Daniël Wijnhorst. Much of this work grew out of BSc and MSc student projects.

Making reading personal

We built an experience around more than simply asking a child questions about a book. Leo got to know each child's interests and reading preferences, helped them find a personally fitting book, and returned to discuss its characters, events, and connections to the child's own life. The sessions also included reading tips, encouragement, humor, and a personalized bookmark with a reading plan that the child helped create.

A central idea emerging from the project was dialogic learning in child–robot interaction: the robot should invite children to explain, interpret, make connections, and express their own views, rather than test them with questions that have only one correct answer. We developed this idea in our AAAI Spring Symposium 2025 paper on dialogic learning.

Combining the creativity of AI with human oversight

To make book-specific conversations feasible at scale, we developed a hybrid human–AI content generation framework. Rather than letting a language model improvise unrestricted conversations with children, we used it selectively to generate personalized dialogue content offline. Automatic checks and human moderation filtered and refined the content before it could be used by Leo within structured, pedagogically motivated interactions. The approach is described in both our initial hybrid-dialogue paper (AAAI 2025) and our more detailed framework paper (AIED 2026).

What did we find?

Together, we designed and ran a four-session study with 101 children aged 8–11 across two primary schools. Compared with book-neutral conversations, personalized book discussions increased children's reading enjoyment and their sense of connection to their book, especially among children whose initial reading enjoyment was not already at its maximum. We did not find an increase in the measured intrinsic reading motivation score. The quantitative effects were no longer detectable at the one-year follow-up, although children still described positive changes in how they saw reading and attributed some of those changes to Leo.

For me, this project demonstrated not just the potential of personalized child–robot conversations, but also what becomes possible when educational design, generative AI, and long-standing collaboration with schools come together.

Beyond a single study

Robot Bookworm also raised an important question: what happens to a personalized child–robot relationship when a year goes by? We returned to explore how Leo could reconnect with children after that long absence, leading to the Robot Reconnect project and, eventually, new questions about children's influence over a robot's memory.

Fitting Humor Age-based personalization

What makes a robot's jokes funny and relatable to children of different ages?

Humor is one way social robots can build rapport with children—but what is funny to one age group may fall completely flat with another. In Fitting Humor, we explored how children's own humor preferences could inform more relatable robot interactions.

102 children in co-design 88 children in evaluation HRI 2025 full paper HRI 2025 Best Paper Honorable Mention

Humor designed with children

We first worked with 102 children aged 8–12 in co-design workshops to explore what kinds of humor different age groups enjoyed. Their drawings, preferences, and ideas informed the jokes Leo would use in personalized conversations.

Does age-fitting make a difference?

We then evaluated the jokes with 88 children. Children generally found age-fitted robot humor more amusing and better matched to their own and their peers' sense of humor. The project highlighted how involving children can improve the design of social personalization.

One lesson has stayed with me: we could not have written these jokes without the children.
